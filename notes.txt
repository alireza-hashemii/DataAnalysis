1. what is the difference between big-endian and little-endian byte ordering?
    Big-endian and little-endian are two ways of ordering the bytes of a multi-byte
    number in memory. Big-endian stores the most significant byte (the "biggest" end)
    first, so `0x12345678` is stored as `12 34 56 78`, just like we write numbers with
    the biggest digit first. Little-endian stores the least significant byte first, 
    so the same number is stored as `78 56 34 12`. Only the order of the bytes is 
    reversed, not the bits inside each byte. Most modern CPUs (x86 and typical ARM)
    use little-endian, while big-endian is common in network protocols, and in NumPy
        `<` means little-endian and `>` means big-endian.


2. what are axis=0 and axis=1 denoting in numpy indexing system? 
    axis=0 is used to address rows(instances) and axis=1 is used to address features(columns)
    of data.


3. what do we mean by 1D/2D array? how the dimension must be interpreted?
    The dimension of an array is the number of axes (independent directions) 
    you need to move along to locate a single element. Equivalently, 
    it's how many indices you must give to pick out one value.

5. what does the method .T (.transpose()) do? 
    it flips rows and columns, so shape (m, n) becomes (n, m)


6. In numpy how do you perform matrix multipication? 
    using @ operator


7. can we claim that dot product is a special form of matrix multipication? 
    The dot product of two vectors is what you get when you matrix-multiply 
    a (1, n) row by an (n, 1) column. The result is a (1, 1) matrix, which 
    we treat as a plain number.
    (1, n) @ (n, 1) -> (1, 1)

8. what is the difference between scalar multipication and element-wise multipication?
    Scalar multiplication multiplies every entry by the same single number. Element-wise
    multiplication multiplies two arrays position by position, so each entry gets its 
    own partner.

10. how different is logarithm compared to exponentiation? 
    Exponentiation and logarithms are inverse operations: 
    one undoes the other. Exponentiation asks "what do I 
    get if I multiply a base by itself this many times?" 
    (2³ = 8), while a logarithm asks the reverse: "how 
    many times must I multiply the base to get this number?"
    (log₂ 8 = 3). So exponentiation takes the base and 
    the exponent and gives you the result, while a logarithm
    takes the base and the result and gives you the exponent.
    In NumPy, that's 2**3 for the first and np.log2(8) for the second.
